BEFORE IDENTITY, ESTABLISH EXISTENCE

The Man History Will Not Let Us Dismiss

Before asking whether Jesus was the Messiah, we should establish something more basic:

Did Jesus actually exist?

The New Testament is the primary collection of ancient documents about His life, but it is not the only historical witness.

ROMAN HISTORIAN

Tacitus

Tacitus wrote that the Christian movement took its name from Christus, who was executed under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius.

JEWISH HISTORIAN

Josephus

Josephus referred to James as “the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ.”

These writers were not composing Christian Scripture.

Their references do not prove Jesus was divine.

They establish a narrower historical foundation: Jesus was a real first-century person whose execution, family, and followers were known outside the New Testament.

Examine the non-Christian sources

Tacitus, Annals 15.44

Tacitus was hostile toward Christianity. His account associates Christus with execution under Pontius Pilate during Tiberius’ reign and describes Christianity spreading to Rome.

These sources do not prove Christianity’s supernatural claims. They make the theory that Jesus was invented centuries later historically difficult to sustain.

History establishes that Jesus existed.

The greater question remains:

Was He the One the Scriptures anticipated?

Could Jesus have planned the fulfillments?

Jesus could deliberately choose certain actions, such as entering Jerusalem on a donkey.

Other circumstances were less open to personal control:

  • His birthplace
  • The rulers in power
  • His opponents’ decisions
  • His manner of execution
  • Events following His death

Intentional fulfillment does not automatically weaken the case. It may reveal that Jesus was consciously claiming to be the promised Messiah. Each event should be weighed individually.

A PROPHECY THAT DEFINES A PERIOD

The Prophecy With a Clock

Daniel 9 records a prayer concerning Jerusalem, Israel’s failures, and the future of God’s people.

The response introduces a prophetic period described as “seventy weeks.”

Understand prophetic time

Historicist interpreters use Numbers 14 and Ezekiel 4 as examples of symbolic days corresponding to years.

This does not mean every mention of a day in prophecy automatically means a year. The interpretation depends on symbolism, scale, and context.

Other interpreters calculate Daniel’s seventy weeks differently. This lesson presents the historicist approach while acknowledging that it is an interpretation rather than an uncontested mathematical rule.

The prophecy now provides a measurable period.

But every timeline needs a starting point.

A DECREE WITH A DATE

When the Clock Began

Daniel says the timeline begins with a command to restore and build Jerusalem.

The historicist interpretation identifies the decree recorded in Ezra 7 because it gave Ezra broad authority to restore Jerusalem’s religious, legal, and civil life.

THE SEVENTH YEAR OF ARTAXERXESEzra 7:7–26Tap to open

7 And there went up some of the children of Israel, and of the priests, and the Levites, and the singers, and the porters, and the Nethinims, unto Jerusalem, in the seventh year of Artaxerxes the king.

25 And thou, Ezra, after the wisdom of thy God, that is in thine hand, set magistrates and judges, which may judge all the people that are beyond the river, all such as know the laws of thy God; and teach ye them that know them not.

26 And whosoever will not do the law of thy God, and the law of the king, let judgment be executed speedily upon him...

Ezra dates the event to the seventh year of King Artaxerxes.

Traditional chronology places this in approximately 458–457 BC. The calculation followed in this guide uses:

START457 BCJerusalem restoration decree
483 years
DESTINATIONAD 27The Anointed One appears

Because there is no year zero between 1 BC and AD 1, counting 483 years from 457 BC reaches AD 27.

Daniel does not say the Messiah would be born at the end of the period.

It points toward the appearance of the Messiah—a word meaning Anointed One.

Why begin in 457 BC?

Several Persian decrees affected Jerusalem and the temple. Historicist interpreters select the decree in Ezra 7 because it authorized the restoration of religious, judicial, and civil administration—not merely construction.

From the historicist perspective, Jesus did not merely appear somewhere in history.

He arrived within the period the prophecy had marked.

Examine the AD 27 chronology

Luke dates John’s ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius. Ancient reigns could be counted from sole rule, an earlier co-regency, through inclusive counting, or according to regional calendars.

Chronologies commonly place Jesus’ baptism between AD 26 and AD 29. The historicist interpretation uses AD 27, which falls within the historically plausible period.

BibleGateway EncyclopediaExplore New Testament chronology ↗

The lesson does not claim that every historian agrees on one exact date. Its case rests on the convergence of Daniel’s period, Persian chronology, and Luke’s historical setting.

Explore other interpretations of Daniel 9

Daniel 9 has produced several interpretations:

  • The historicist timeline culminating in Jesus
  • Christian messianic readings using different dates
  • Views centered on Antiochus IV
  • Futurist interpretations separating the final week
  • Jewish interpretations that do not identify the anointed figure with Jesus

The phrase translated “Messiah the Prince” can also be rendered “an anointed one, a prince.”

This lesson presents the historicist Christian case because it sees a coherent connection among Jerusalem’s restoration, the 483-year period, Jesus’ baptism, and His anointing by the Holy Spirit.

HOW MUCH WEIGHT SHOULD MULTIPLE PREDICTIONS CARRY?

More Than an Impressive Coincidence

Peter W. Stoner and Robert C. Newman popularized a calculation concerning eight selected messianic prophecies.

THE PUBLISHED ESTIMATE 1 IN 1017 One in one hundred quadrillion

The number is memorable.

But it should be treated as an illustration—not unquestionable scientific proof.

Probability estimates depend on which passages are selected, how they are interpreted, whether events are independent, and how the original odds are assigned.

Daniel 9 offers a different kind of argument.

It presents an identifiable starting event, a defined period, a historical destination, and a public anointing in Jesus’ life.

Test the probability argument

Stoner and Newman applied probability estimates to selected biblical prophecies in Science Speaks.

For eight prophecies, the published estimate was one in 1017. For forty-eight, Stoner extended it to one in 10157.

The assigned probabilities were estimates rather than measured statistics. Questions include:

  • Were the selected events independent?
  • Were the original odds realistic?
  • Which passages should count as messianic?
  • Could some events be deliberately influenced?

The calculation is best used as an illustration of cumulative evidence—not as mathematically indisputable proof.

Science Speaks online editionRead the original argument ↗

The case does not require exaggerated certainty.

It invites careful examination.

And that leads to the question evidence alone cannot answer for you:

What will you do with Jesus?

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE CHRONOLOGY

He Came on Time. What Will You Do With Him?

It is possible to study Jesus while keeping Him safely distant.

You can analyze dates, compare sources, and debate interpretations without allowing the conclusion to affect the life you have built.

But Daniel’s prophecy is not ultimately about winning a chronology argument.

End sin Make reconciliation Bring righteousness Reveal the Anointed One

Those are not only historical ideas.

They address personal needs.

You may have built an admirable life. You may be capable, respected, and disciplined.

But achievement cannot reconcile you to God or create everlasting righteousness within you.

The prophecy points to a Person—not merely a date.

Jesus came at the right time in history. Perhaps this study has reached you at the right time in your life.

When investigation becomes distance

Careful investigation is valuable. But analysis can also become a sophisticated way to avoid commitment.

A person can continually request one more source, one more argument, one more calculation, or one more explanation.

Sometimes that is intellectual honesty. Sometimes it is fear disguised as caution.

Jesus does not ask for blind belief. But evidence eventually brings us to a decision.

At some point the question changes from “Is there enough evidence to keep investigating?” to “What am I protecting by keeping Jesus at a distance?”
